3) First-order expansion (the “first variation”)

Consider a trajectory \(q(t)\) and a small deviation \(\delta q(t)\). The corresponding velocity deviation is \(\delta \dot{q}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\delta q(t)\). A first-order multivariable Taylor expansion of the Lagrangian around \((q,\dot{q},t)\) yields

$$ L\!\big(q+\delta q,\ \dot{q}+\delta \dot{q},\ t\big) = L(q,\dot{q},t) + \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\,\delta q + \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}}\,\delta \dot{q} + \mathcal{O}\!\big((\delta q)^2\big). $$

Substituting this expansion into the difference of actions, \(S[q+\delta q]-S[q]\), and keeping only the terms that are linear in the deviation, one obtains

$$ S[q+\delta q]-S[q] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\,\delta q + \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}}\,\delta \dot{q} \right)\,dt + \mathcal{O}\!\big((\delta q)^2\big). $$

Definition. The first variation \(\delta S\) is precisely the linear part in \(\delta q\) and \(\delta \dot{q}\):

$$ \delta S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\,\delta q + \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}}\,\delta \dot{q} \right)\,dt. $$

In a purely geometric way: there is a change in the action that is proportional to the deviation. The change presumably makes the action greater; otherwise we haven’t got a minimum. But then if the change is proportional to the deviation, reversing the sign of the deviation will make the action less. We would get the action to increase one way and to decrease the other way.

This is the basic reason why, at an extremum (minimum, maximum, or saddle in function space), the linear term must vanish: for a true minimum, the leading nonzero contribution cannot be odd under \(\delta q \mapsto -\delta q\). In practice, one enforces this by requiring \(\delta S=0\) for all admissible variations \(\delta q(t)\) (typically with \(\delta q(t_1)=\delta q(t_2)=0\)), and the Euler–Lagrange equation follows after an integration by parts.
