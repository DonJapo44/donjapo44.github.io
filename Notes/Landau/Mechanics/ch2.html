Chapter 2. Conservation Laws

March 15, 2026

One of the most eye-opening and intellectually satisfying aspects of this chapter is the way the conservation laws emerge directly from symmetry principles. It is quite elegant to see that conservation of energy follows from the homogeneity of time, that linear momentum follows as a conservation law from the homogeneity of space, and that angular momentum follows as a conservation law from the isotropy of space. In this way, Landau presents the basic structure of mechanics not as a list of separate facts, but as a unified consequence of the symmetries of nature.

Euler’s theorem for homogeneous functions

Before discussing conservation laws, it is useful to recall Euler’s theorem for homogeneous functions. Let \(f(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) be a differentiable function homogeneous of degree \(k\), meaning that

$$ f(\lambda x_1,\dots,\lambda x_n)=\lambda^k f(x_1,\dots,x_n). $$

Define the auxiliary one-variable function

$$ g(\lambda)=f(\lambda x_1,\dots,\lambda x_n). $$

By homogeneity, this can also be written as

$$ g(\lambda)=\lambda^k f(x_1,\dots,x_n). $$

Differentiating this expression with respect to \(\lambda\), one obtains

$$ g'(\lambda)=k\lambda^{k-1}f(x_1,\dots,x_n). $$

On the other hand, applying the chain rule directly to \(g(\lambda)=f(\lambda x_1,\dots,\lambda x_n)\) gives

$$ g'(\lambda) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\lambda x_1,\dots,\lambda x_n). $$

Since both expressions represent the same derivative, they must be equal. Therefore,

$$ \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\lambda x_1,\dots,\lambda x_n) = k\lambda^{k-1}f(x_1,\dots,x_n). $$

Evaluating at \(\lambda=1\), one arrives at

$$ \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x_1,\dots,x_n) = k f(x_1,\dots,x_n). $$

Hence,

$$ \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}=k f, $$

which is precisely Euler’s theorem for homogeneous functions.

Euler’s theorem applied to the kinetic energy

In mechanics, the kinetic energy

$$ T=T(\dot{q}_1,\dots,\dot{q}_n) $$

is a homogeneous function of degree \(2\) in the generalized velocities. Therefore, by Euler’s theorem,

$$ \sum_i \dot{q}_i\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot{q}_i}=2T. $$

Momentum

Consider an infinitesimal translation of the whole system,

$$ \mathbf{r}_a \to \mathbf{r}_a+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, $$

so that each position changes by \(\delta \mathbf{r}_a=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\). Since \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) is constant, the velocities do not change:

$$ \delta \dot{\mathbf{r}}_a=0. $$

The first-order Taylor expansion of the Lagrangian then gives

$$ \delta L = \sum_a \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{r}_a}\cdot \delta \mathbf{r}_a + \sum_a \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}}_a}\cdot \delta \dot{\mathbf{r}}_a. $$

Since \(\delta \dot{\mathbf{r}}_a=0\), this reduces to

$$ \delta L = \sum_a \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{r}_a}\cdot \boldsymbol{\epsilon}. $$

If space is homogeneous, a global translation cannot change the physics, and therefore \(\delta L=0\). Because \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) is arbitrary, it follows that

$$ \sum_a \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{r}_a}=0. $$

Landau then uses the Lagrange equations,

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}}_a}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{r}_a} = 0, $$

to replace \(\partial L/\partial \mathbf{r}_a\) by a time derivative:

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{r}_a} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}}_a}\right). $$

Substituting this into the previous result, one obtains

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\sum_a \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}}_a}\right)=0. $$

Thus, the sum of the conjugate momenta is conserved. This quantity is the total linear momentum, defined by

$$ \mathbf{P} = \sum_a \mathbf{p}_a = \sum_a \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}}_a}. $$

Angular Momentum

Consider an infinitesimal rotation by \(\delta \boldsymbol{\phi}\). The corresponding variations of the position and velocity vectors are

$$ \delta \mathbf{r}_a=\delta \boldsymbol{\phi}\times \mathbf{r}_a, \qquad \delta \mathbf{v}_a=\delta \boldsymbol{\phi}\times \mathbf{v}_a. $$

The first-order variation of the Lagrangian is therefore

$$ \delta L = \sum_a \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{r}_a}\cdot \delta \mathbf{r}_a + \sum_a \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{v}_a}\cdot \delta \mathbf{v}_a. $$

Using the definitions

$$ \mathbf{p}_a=\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{v}_a}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{r}_a}=\dot{\mathbf{p}}_a, $$

this becomes

$$ \delta L = \delta \boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot \sum_a \left( \mathbf{r}_a\times \dot{\mathbf{p}}_a + \mathbf{v}_a\times \mathbf{p}_a \right). $$

Now observe that

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\big(\mathbf{r}_a\times \mathbf{p}_a\big) = \mathbf{v}_a\times \mathbf{p}_a + \mathbf{r}_a\times \dot{\mathbf{p}}_a. $$

Therefore,

$$ \delta L = \delta \boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot \frac{d}{dt} \sum_a \big(\mathbf{r}_a\times \mathbf{p}_a\big). $$

If space is isotropic, then \(\delta L=0\) for any infinitesimal rotation. It follows that

$$ \frac{d}{dt} \sum_a \big(\mathbf{r}_a\times \mathbf{p}_a\big) = 0. $$

Hence, the conserved quantity is the total angular momentum,

$$ \mathbf{M} = \sum_a \mathbf{r}_a\times \mathbf{p}_a. $$

Mechanical Similarity

The derivation of the mechanical-similarity relations begins by scaling the trajectory and the time variable according to

$$ \mathbf{r}'=\alpha \mathbf{r}, \qquad t'=\beta t. $$

Under this transformation, the velocity changes as

$$ \mathbf{v}' = \frac{d\mathbf{r}'}{dt'} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\,\mathbf{v}. $$

Therefore, the kinetic energy scales as

$$ T'\sim (\mathbf{v}')^2 = \left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^2 T. $$

Suppose now that the potential is homogeneous of degree \(k\), that is,

$$ U(\alpha \mathbf{r})=\alpha^k U(\mathbf{r}). $$

Then the potential energy transforms as

$$ U'=\alpha^k U. $$

For the transformed motion to satisfy equations of the same form, the kinetic and potential terms must scale with the same factor. Hence,

$$ \left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^2=\alpha^k. $$

Solving for \(\beta\), one obtains

$$ \beta=\alpha^{\,1-k/2}. $$

Since \(\alpha=l'/l\), it follows that

$$ \frac{t'}{t} = \left(\frac{l'}{l}\right)^{1-k/2}. $$

Next, because \(v\sim l/t\), the velocity scales as

$$ \frac{v'}{v} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta} = \alpha^{k/2} = \left(\frac{l'}{l}\right)^{k/2}. $$

The total energy scales in the same way as either \(T\) or \(U\), and therefore

$$ \frac{E'}{E} = \alpha^k = \left(\frac{l'}{l}\right)^k. $$

Finally, since the angular momentum behaves as \(M\sim rv\), one finds

$$ \frac{M'}{M} = \alpha\cdot \alpha^{k/2} = \alpha^{1+k/2} = \left(\frac{l'}{l}\right)^{1+k/2}. $$

These are the mechanical-similarity relations. They show how time, velocity, energy, and angular momentum must transform when the coordinates are rescaled in a system whose potential is a homogeneous function of degree \(k\).
