Film · Directed by James Watkins · 2026

Clayface

October 27, 2026 ⚠ Spoilers
Dr. Caitlyn Corr and Matt Hagen in Clayface (2026). Image © FilmAffinity. Used for commentary and analysis.

Magnificent film with the right recipe: tragedy, a love story, revenge and gore. Once again, Hollywood haunts us with AI gone wrong, this time through A.I. robots called nanocells. They work so well and so efficiently that they are capable of reacting to Matt Hagen’s emotional state, and the movie turns this into clever somatic metaphors. For example, the nanocell treatment closes Matt Hagen’s eyes, because he was blind to the fact that his agent, Frank Fitzpatrick, was scamming him through his ties to the mob.

To quote Kate DiCamillo, “Every good story is fundamentally a love story.” I really enjoyed how the movie uses the fact that the only person who can calm Matt is Dr. Corr, which is one of the reasons he falls in love with her. There is a popular saying in Spanish, el amor y el dinero no se pueden ocultar (“love and money cannot be hidden”), and the scene where Matt dreams of her and transforms into Dr. Corr proves it. I gasped in astonishment; just like Dr. Corr, I was speechless.

I tip my hat to the way this movie builds tension, starting with the foreshadowing in its first lines, when Matt says his agent is bleeding him dry. Then comes the deceit, as Corey tries to hide that he is the one feeding stories from Matt’s past to Frank Fitzpatrick. At the end of the movie, when Dr. Corr realizes that Matt is also a shapeshifter, the paranoid tension evokes Carpenter’s The Thing, where a nervous audience tries to anticipate who might be the monster disguised as another human. Another remarkable scene comes at the very end, when a beggar approaches Dr. Corr and her kindness and empathy toward him break the hypervigilant state she is in. Last but not least, the love triangle between Detective John Borrelli, Dr. Corr and Matt Hagen is brilliant; I enjoyed how Borrelli senses that Dr. Corr’s relationship with Matt has gone beyond doctor and patient.

One of the main themes of the movie is the consequences of small acts that seem harmless at first: Matt’s decision to dance with Mia Torrez, the mobster’s girlfriend, despite being warned, and Dr. Corr and Matt’s decision to go ahead with the treatment even though it was too early for human trials. The movie also touches on current themes beyond AI, such as defamation, when Mia lies to the press about the incident at the club where she danced with Matt.

By the way, I am thrilled with this movie’s soundtrack. Goldfrapp’s “Ooh La La” is a fitting club song, and Chicago’s “If You Leave Me Now” is another perfect choice for the first nanocell treatment, as relaxing as Dr. Corr promises. Volker Bertelmann’s original score is just right, creating an atmosphere that is both tragic and haunting.

This movie has become my favorite villain origin story. A day after watching it, I am still thinking about what an enjoyable experience it was. The last time a story left me feeling this way was when I first read Macbeth: the slow, inevitable making of a monster, a man who sheds every trace of his morality on his way to moral ruin. Clayface carries those same stakes. It is a true tragedy, one in which a man is stripped of all he holds dear (his face, his love, his friendship) and still keeps falling, until all that remains is the monster.

Chef’s kiss. 👨‍🍳🤌
