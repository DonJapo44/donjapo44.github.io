Coyote vs. Acme

August 31, 2026 ⚠ Spoilers
Coyote using skates from Coyote vs. Acme. © Ketchup Entertainment or related entities; used for commentary and analysis.

A satisfying film, and one with a rebellious undertone: at heart this is a movie about protest. There isn't a boring scene in it, and Wile E. Coyote turns out to have a soft side, one that values friendship.

The target is Acme, an enormous and frankly evil corporation that exploits its users' data and knowingly sells defective products. The parallel with our own situation is not subtle, and it isn't meant to be.

The underdog, or perhaps the undercoyote, ultimately loses the lawsuit. That is the point. The lesson here is perseverance rather than achievement: not giving up. The film's own history reinforces it, having been nearly shelved by Warner Bros. and rescued by fans who refused to let it disappear.

Another keen touch is Dr. Lorre, who functions as the whistleblower on Acme's practices. In real life Peter Lorre walked off The Verdict in 1945 in support of a Hollywood strike.

The relationship between the Road Runner and the Coyote also gets explored, and it becomes a question of identity. The Road Runner admits the Coyote is a friend to him, even as the Coyote keeps chasing. It seems he will always run and the Coyote will never catch him. It reminded me of another Warner Bros. film, The Dark Knight, where the Joker explains that he has no interest in killing Batman: What would I do without you? And later: Do I really look like a guy with a plan? You know what I am? I'm a dog chasing cars. I wouldn't know what to do with one if I caught it! You know, I just... do things. Part of the obsession with chasing is precisely how hard the thing is to catch.

A recommended gem, blending complex animation with live actors. Satisfying, with a sprinkle of protest. I saw it on a Sunday during opening week; the theater was packed and there were no children in it. Audiences and themes evolve, while some cartoon characters never get old.
