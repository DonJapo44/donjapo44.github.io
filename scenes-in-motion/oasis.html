Oasis: Don't Look Back in Anger

⚠ Spoilers
Oasis performing at Principality Stadium, Cardiff. Wikimedia Commons. Photo: Raph_PH, via Flickr. Used for commentary and analysis.

What an enjoyable experience, full of shifting emotions. Liam remarks that when Oasis split in 2009, the world got a little weird. I'd add that music got a little weird too. I miss the days when bands from the nineties and early 2000s could put anger, hope, loss, anxiety, and desperation into three minutes and mean every second of it.

This is a film best felt rather than watched, letting each song reopen exactly what it meant to you the first time it mattered. It struck me, watching it, how much of that came from anger, addiction, and the drinking that shadowed some of the band's members. And yet Oasis also wrote some of the most hopeful, melancholic, beautiful lines in modern music. My favorite of their songs is Champagne Supernova. Its opening verses got me thinking about friendship, and how differently it's felt moving from adolescence into adulthood. And I find real depth in how the song closes: a quiet promise that love, between the right two people, simply doesn't die.

I finally understood today how Liam's raspy voice and that slightly tilted-back way of singing landed with me the way it does. It's a current of negative emotion run through with a hope of healing and moving forward once a loss has been processed. Looking back now, after more than a decade, I realize how much Stop Crying Your Heart Out personally helped me through depression from those years. It's striking how Don't Look Back in Anger became exactly the right song after the 2017 Manchester terror attack. When I try to reason out what touches people this deeply about this band, I keep coming back to Wittgenstein's Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus: the secret is bound up in emotions and specific moments of our own growth that words can't quite reach, or that we've forgotten in detail while never losing the feeling of how they landed. That's what made this band so massive, and it's why England's football team was still singing Wonderwall at the end of matches at the 2026 World Cup.

The documentary's score and its slow-motion shots set to Tchaikovsky's Swan Lake bring back all those years we felt the band was gone for good. Everyone got older across those sixteen years, and with that came the itch for a reunion.

Delightful documentary about a band that connected with millions of souls.
