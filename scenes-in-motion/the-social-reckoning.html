Film · Directed by Aaron Sorkin · 2026

The Social Reckoning

October 26, 2026 ⚠ Spoilers
Aaron Sorkin and Mikey Madison on the set of The Social Reckoning (2026). Image © IMDb. Used for commentary and analysis.

Enjoyable movie, though it loses the magic of the first one, which explored far more themes: competition, betrayal among friends, and more. Still, several reflections emerge from it. The opening scene is memorable: Kayleigh, a blonde woman pulled over drunk in California, launches into a racist rant at the police officers until she gets arrested.

Mikey Madison is delightful as Frances Haugen, and her performance carries the deep ethical dilemmas the movie raises about the tech industry we live in today. Her strong belief in democracy is challenged when Facebook’s algorithm fuels dangerous political polarization and exploits users through manipulation, simply because it makes them engage more with the platform. Jeremy Allen White is wonderful as Jeff Horwitz, a reporter troubled and upset by the lack of accountability in his profession. As he puts it, the problem is not that everyone has a platform to share ideas, news and thoughts; the problem is bad reporting and its harmful consequences.

Free speech is not free if, underneath it, users’ emotions and data are being manipulated and exploited. The movie shows one of the political and social consequences of this: Kayleigh ends up taking part in the January 6, 2021 insurrection.

I believe the movie’s biggest flaw is that Jeremy Strong is not present enough. He plays Mark Zuckerberg brilliantly, socially awkward, obsessed with competition and cold in his decision-making. But his character only has one gear. The movie also suffers from constantly reminding us of the first one, The Social Network, which had far richer emotional swings.

I do not know whether the state of the world is simply that troubling, or whether not enough time has passed since the events it portrays, but I believe the audience already knew the main ideas and criticisms this movie wants to debate.

A pleasing movie about recent events, with a masterful Aaron Sorkin as captain of the ship, and about how Meta has turned into a darker platform. Cinematically, though, I did not pump my fist at the end, the way Frances does when, for the first time, she cannot remember a moment she felt so happy.
